# Choosing a Home Coffee Roaster

Roasting your own coffee at home is one of the most rewarding ways to explore the full range of what coffee has to offer. But before you buy a roaster, it helps to understand that not all roasters work the same way — and the type of roaster you choose will shape the kind of coffee you produce.

This guide is written for home roasters. It covers the three main categories of roasters available to enthusiasts, the practical factors that should influence your decision, and perhaps the most personal factor of all: matching a roaster to the kind of coffee you enjoy drinking.

## The Three Roaster Categories

Coffee roasters transfer heat to green coffee beans using different methods. The way heat is delivered — and how beans move during roasting — affects the flavor, body, and clarity of the final cup. There are three broad categories relevant to home roasting.

### Fluid Bed Roasters

Fluid bed roasters — also called air roasters — use a stream of hot air to both heat and agitate the beans. The beans are suspended in the airflow, similar to how popcorn pops in an air popper. This means convection is the primary method of heat transfer.

Because the beans are in constant motion and heated primarily by air, fluid bed roasters are often associated with coffees that highlight origin character and clarity. The rapid, even heat transfer minimizes the risk of scorching and tipping. That said, the roast profile you choose matters far more than the machine type — skilled roasters produce the full spectrum of flavors on fluid bed roasters.

**Examples:** FreshRoast SR500/SR800, LINK (by Kaffelogic/Nucleus)

**Source documentation:**
- [Sivetz — The Original Fluid Bed Roaster](https://sivetz.com/)
- [Sweet Maria's Coffee Library — Roasting Resources](https://library.sweetmarias.com/)

### Hybrid Roasters

Hybrid roasters are designed to combine the efficiency of air roasting with the even bean movement of a drum. They are often highly automated and built for consistency, making them popular among competition baristas and roasteries that value repeatability.

**Examples:** Roest S100/L100, Stronghold S7 series

**Source documentation:**
- [Roest Coffee — Hybrid Sample Roasters](https://www.roestcoffee.com/)
- [Stronghold Technology — Smart Roasting Platform](https://stronghold.coffee/)

### Drum Roasters

Drum roasters are the most traditional design. Beans rotate inside a heated drum, receiving heat through both **conduction** (contact with the hot drum surface) and **convection** (hot air passing through the drum). The combination of conductive and convective heat gives drum roasters a wide range of expressiveness — they are capable of producing everything from bright, clean, acidic profiles to deep, caramelized, body-forward roasts, depending on how they are operated. They offer a wide range of control but generally have a steeper learning curve.

**Examples:** Behmor 1600 Plus, Aillio Bullet R1, Kaleido Sniper series

**Source documentation:**
- [Aillio Bullet R1 V2](https://us.aillio.com/products/bullet-r1-v2)
- [Kaleido Sniper Coffee Roasters](https://kaleidoroasters.com/)

## Decision Factors

### Cost

Home roasters span a wide price range. Here is how the market generally breaks down:

- **Entry tier (~$200–$800):** Machines like the FreshRoast SR800 and Behmor 1600 Plus. These are great for learning the basics and producing good coffee at home without a major investment.
- **Mid-range (~$1,000–$2,500):** Machines like the Kaleido Sniper M-series, the LINK, and the Hottop KN-8828B-2K+. These offer significantly more control, better build quality, and software support.
- **High-end prosumer (~$4,000–$17,000+):** Machines like the Aillio Bullet R2, Stronghold S7 Pro, and Roest sample roasters. These approach (or reach) commercial-grade performance and are used by competition baristas and professional roasters. Note that this tier has drifted upward: the Bullet's current models start around $4,100, and the Stronghold S7 Pro is a five-figure machine.

The right choice depends on how serious you are about roasting, how much coffee you plan to roast, and whether you value automation or hands-on control.

### Workflow and Batch Size

How much coffee do you roast in a typical session? This is one of the most practical questions to answer before buying.

**Batch size considerations:**
- If you drink one or two cups a day, a roaster that handles 100–200g per batch is sufficient.
- If you roast for a household or share with friends, you may want 300g–1kg capacity.
- Larger batches mean fewer roasting sessions per week, but the machines that handle them tend to be more expensive.

**Electrical constraints on 110V/120V:**
In the US, standard household outlets (110V/120V) are limited to about 1,500–1,800W. This has a real impact on fluid bed roasters, which rely entirely on electrical heating elements. On 110V/120V, a fluid bed roaster may need to run smaller batches to maintain adequate bean agitation and heat transfer. Drum roasters are less affected because the drum retains heat and continues to transfer it even when the heating element cycles.

Some roasters offer 240V configurations. While 240V is less common in US homes, it is becoming more accessible as electric vehicle chargers are installed in garages. A 240V circuit roughly doubles the available power, which can significantly improve batch size and heat responsiveness — particularly for fluid bed and hybrid roasters. If you have access to 240V (or are willing to install a dedicated circuit), it expands your options considerably.

**Other workflow factors:**
- **Cool-down time:** How quickly does the roaster cool beans after the roast? Faster cooling means you can roast back-to-back batches.
- **Chaff management:** Fluid bed roasters collect chaff in a separate basket. Drum roasters may require more frequent cleaning.
- **Smoke:** Darker roasts produce more smoke. Some roasters (like the Behmor 1600 Plus) include built-in smoke suppression, which matters if you roast indoors.

### Automation and Software Support

**What is roast profiling?**

Roast profiling is the practice of recording and reproducing the temperature curve of a roast over time. A roast profile typically tracks:

- **Bean temperature (BT):** The temperature of the coffee beans themselves.
- **Environmental temperature (ET):** The temperature of the air inside the roasting chamber.
- **Rate of rise (RoR):** How quickly the bean temperature is increasing, measured in degrees per minute.

Why does this matter? Because a great roast is not just about hitting a certain color or time — it is about controlling the *rate* at which heat is applied. A well-managed RoR curve helps you avoid underdeveloped or baked coffees, and it lets you reproduce a roast you loved last week. Profiling turns roasting from guesswork into a repeatable process.

**The software ecosystem:**

- **[Artisan](https://artisan-scope.org/)** — Free, open-source roast profiling software. Widely supported across many roaster brands. Requires a computer and, in some cases, additional hardware (thermocouples, Phidgets) to connect. Highly customizable but has a learning curve.
- **[Cropster](https://cropster.com/)** — Commercial-grade roast profiling and production management software. Used by many professional roasteries. Offers a home roaster tier. More polished than Artisan but comes with a subscription cost.
- **[RoastWorld](https://roast.world/)** — Cloud-based platform for logging, sharing, and comparing roast profiles. Native to the Aillio Bullet ecosystem but accessible to other roasters. Strong community features.
- **Built-in apps** — Many modern roasters include their own software:
  - **Aillio RoasTime** — Native software for the Bullet R1, with profile logging and playback.
  - **Stronghold Roastware** — Embedded software with program roasting, manual mode, and profile sharing via Stronghold Square.
  - **Roest Connect** — Web portal for Roest roasters with profile library and remote control.
  - **LINK App / LINK Studio** — Mobile app and desktop software for the LINK roaster, with 205+ preloaded profiles and live tracking.
  - **Kaleido Intelligent Roasting System** — Touchscreen interface with profile saving and Artisan compatibility on Pro models.

## The Final Factor: Matching a Roaster to Your Palate

Cost, workflow, and software support are all important. But there is one factor that is more personal and harder to quantify: **the kind of coffee you like to drink.**

Different roaster types use different methods of heat transfer, and the roasters chosen by specialty coffee professionals can give us a sense of what each category is capable of. Below are documented examples of commercial roasters using each category, with links to their own statements about the equipment and the profiles it produces.

It is important to emphasize that **the roaster does not determine the cup** — your roast profile, green coffee selection, and skill matter far more. Any roaster type can produce bright, clean, acidic coffees or deep, caramelized, body-forward roasts. What differs between categories is how heat is delivered and how the machine responds to your adjustments. Looking at what professionals achieve on each type can help you understand the range of possibilities.

> **How to read the sources below.** Equipment endorsements in specialty coffee come overwhelmingly from manufacturers' own customer-story pages, and they are not neutral. We have labeled each source as **vendor** (manufacturer marketing), **independent** (trade press, third-party reporting), or **self-published** (the roaster's own blog). Where a professional's competition title is cited, we verified it against official competition results rather than the vendor's caption — several vendor pages get these wrong. The most valuable sources here are the ones where a roaster published detailed measurements with a disclosure of no financial relationship, such as Christopher Feran's Roest comparison.

### Fluid Bed Roasters in Practice

**Documented commercial examples:**

- **[Ember Coffee Co.](https://embercoffee.co/) (Big Lake, Minnesota)** — An all-fluid-bed roastery, named Sivetz Roaster of the Month in June. Notably, they have **never owned a drum roaster**: they started on a hand-upgraded Coffee Crafters Artisan XE/XL with a Hermethius control module and later added a Sivetz for volume, not for method. In their own words: "We have just found this way easier to be consistent on, and consistency is most of the job." They are careful to hedge the flavor claim themselves — "plenty of drum roasters make clean, sweet coffee too." - **[Lake Geneva Coffee Roastery](https://www.lgcrcoffee.com/) (Delavan, Wisconsin)** — Uses a Sivetz **SRM15** fluid bed roaster. ### Drum Roasters in Practice

**Documented commercial examples:**

- **[Onyx Coffee Lab](https://onyxcoffeelab.com/) (Arkansas)** — Onyx runs a deliberately mixed fleet and matches the machine to the coffee. In their own words: *"Where sweetness and body are more desired, we will enlist our predominantly conductive heat with our two Diedrichs. More delicate coffees showcasing a bright acidity are best served with a convective system, such as our Loring 70kg machine. Our most exotic offerings require incomparable clarity and balance; something we can achieve using the radiant heat transfer provided by our cutting-edge Stronghold S7 roasters."* Individual product pages name the specific production roaster used for each coffee. On process: *"When coffees arrive at our roastery, we meticulously refine each coffee's roast profile with precision, ensuring every cup highlights the coffee's natural brilliance."* [Source: Onyx Coffee Lab](https://onyxcoffeelab.com/)

  > **Note:** This guide previously said Onyx roasts on "rebuilt Diedrich IR-12 and IR-40" machines. That was true historically — Daily Coffee News documented the IR-12 and a refurbished IR-40 in 2017 — but the IR-40 was sold in 2021, and Onyx's current pages reference Diedrich **CR-35** machines instead. The IR-12/IR-40 description survives online mainly through a stale third-party press blurb that Onyx reprints on its own homepage. Onyx is also a useful illustration of the guide's central point: the same roastery uses conduction-heavy, convection-heavy, and radiant machines to pursue different outcomes from different coffees.

- **[Tim Wendelboe](https://www.timwendelboe.no/) (Oslo, Norway)** — Roasts production on a **Loring S35 Kestrel (35 kg)**, having previously used a German Probat that now lives at Fuglen Coffee Roasters. For sample roasting he uses a ROEST, praising its ability to cool coffee during roasting — *"Getting the coffee to cool down fast is very important, otherwise it's going to taste flat and bitter"* — and to save farm-specific profiles: *"you make the specific profile for the specific farm and it works for all their coffees."* Known for very light roasting and direct trade relationships. [Source: Tim Wendelboe (own site)](https://timwendelboe.no/2019/09/whats-cropping-up-sept-2019/) · [Source: ROEST Blog](https://www.roestcoffee.com/blog/tim-wendelboe-sample-roasting) (manufacturer testimonial)

  > **Three corrections worth flagging.** First, the Loring S35 is a **35-kilogram** machine (~77 lb), not "35-pound" — that error comes from a unit slip in a widely-cited retailer blog post. Second, the machine appears to date to **2018**, not 2019. Third, and most importantly: **a Loring is not really a drum roaster.** Its drum is paddle-agitated rather than tumbling against a hot wall, and it transfers heat almost entirely by convection. Scott Rao — cited above in this same guide — writes that "although IMF and Loring machines have drums, most of us call them 'air roasters' because they transfer heat almost exclusively by convection with little-to-no conduction." Wendelboe is listed in this section because he is a well-documented example of a professional's production setup, but by heat-transfer mechanism his Loring belongs closer to the fluid bed and hybrid categories than to the Probats and Diedrichs alongside it.

- **[Glitch Coffee & Roasters](https://glitchcoffee.com/) (Tokyo, Japan)** — Roasts on a 5kg Probat drum roaster visible in the shop floor in Jimbōchō. Owner Kiyokazu Suzuki runs a share-roasting program on the machine, hosting other café owners who want to roast on it. Known for extremely light roasts and high clarity. [Source](https://www.brian-coffee-spot.com/2019/09/19/glitch-coffee-roasters/)

- **The broader specialty industry** — The vast majority of well-known specialty roasters (Stumptown, Counter Culture, Intelligentsia, Blue Bottle, and many others) roast on machines from manufacturers like Probat, Giesen, Diedrich, and Loring. Drum roasters are the industry standard not because they produce one specific flavor profile, but because they offer a wide range of expressiveness — a skilled roaster on a drum can produce bright, clean, acidic coffees alongside deep, caramelized, body-forward roasts. (As noted above, Loring machines are conventionally grouped with this set by industry familiarity, but they are convection-dominant air roasters by mechanism.)

### How to Think About This

The roaster type is one variable among many. What matters most is your roast profile — how you manage heat application, airflow, and development time. That said, different roaster types respond differently to your adjustments:

- **Fluid bed roasters** respond quickly to changes in fan speed and heat. The convective-only heat transfer means less risk of scorching, which some roasters find forgiving when working with dense, high-altitude beans.
- **Hybrid roasters** often come with built-in automation and profile libraries, which can help you achieve consistent results while you are still learning. The combination of heat sources gives you flexibility in how you approach different coffees.
- **Drum roasters** offer the widest range of control and the steepest learning curve. The combination of conductive and convective heat means you have more levers to pull — and more opportunities to shape the roast to your preferences.

**A note of caution:** The roaster type influences how heat is delivered, but it does not lock you into a specific flavor profile. A skilled roaster can produce bright, clean, acidic coffees on a drum roaster and deep, caramelized roasts on a fluid bed. Your green coffee selection, roast profile, and technique matter far more than the machine category. The goal here is not to say one type is better — it is to help you understand which type might feel most natural for the way you want to roast.
